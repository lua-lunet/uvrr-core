## Abstract

Small replicated metadata should not require a disk flush on the critical path. Unbounded Viewstamped Replication Revisited is a protocol design for embedding strong consistency with volatile normal-path replication and crash-stop reincarnation. A cleanly stopped process restarts with its identity and flushed state. A crashed process permanently retires its old identity and rejoins under a fresh identity, obtaining state and voting authority through reconfiguration. Its organising mechanism is leader overlap: where the leader is the sole intersection of the active decision quorum and the next prepare quorum, it withholds its own promise so that preparation can proceed while the active quorum remains usable. Five-voter replacement uses six committed batches, including exact doubling and halving. A constructive Rust solver also computes paths between arbitrary weighted-majority configurations with an available majority at each endpoint. The Lean development proves weighted reachability and a phantom-identity casting-vote construction, alongside agreement and identity exclusion for a three-node schedule and its five-voter extension. The addendum published with the code separates the proof contracts and executable checks.

*Keywords: Crash-stop, leader overlap, reconfiguration, strong consistency, Viewstamped Replication.*

## I. Introduction

Oki and Liskov introduced Viewstamped Replication in 1988 [1]. Liskov and Cowling's 2012 Viewstamped Replication Revisited (VRR) eliminated disk writes during normal processing and view changes [2]. Replication therefore retained the state needed for agreement without adding a local disk flush to each update. The remaining difficulty was how to bring a replica back after loss of that state.

Michael *et al.* exposed a safety failure in VRR's recovery mechanism in 2016 [3, Sec. 4.2 and Appendix A.1]. A replica can send a view-change commitment, crash, and return in an older view having forgotten the commitment. It can then help commit an operation that a later leader loses when delayed view-change messages arrive. Their counterexample breaks linearizability, including when channels preserve message order. The authors provide a virtual stable storage construction to support diskless crash recovery. Their result makes the problem precise: restoring a replica's data must also preserve the commitments made by its identity.

Unbounded Viewstamped Replication Revisited (uVRR) repairs this failure by making a crash final for the protocol identity. The crashed identity never resumes execution. The process that replaces it joins under a fresh identity, receives state from the cluster, and acquires voting authority through committed membership changes. Messages sent before the crash remain messages of the old identity; the new process cannot add fresh votes to that identity's history. A cleanly stopped process can instead restart under its existing identity because it has preserved its protocol state. Startup fencing and clean shutdown use durable writes, keeping those writes outside normal replication. Loss of volatile state therefore leads to replacement of a member: crash recovery becomes crash-stop reincarnation.

Turner supplies the construction that connects this lifecycle to continued replication [5]. Where the leader is the sole intersection of an active decision quorum and the next prepare quorum, it can withhold its own promise while collecting the others. The active quorum remains usable during that preparation; the leader completes the handover with its own local promise. We use this leader overlap to organise reincarnation. For three unit-weight voters, replacement takes two committed batches. For five, the weighted schedule has six, including exact doubling and halving. The addendum published with the code [16] gives the proof ladder, a general configuration-path constructor and an abstract casting-vote completion using fresh identities. Turner's agreement argument supplies the basis for composing safe adjacent configuration boundaries.

Together, these ideas retain VRR's diskless normal path while giving failed members a route back through fresh identities. The intended setting is agreement over small amounts of metadata embedded in a distributed application [6]. Such a service can coordinate membership or advisory-lock metadata without putting synchronous persistence on every update's critical path. Its normal replication need not wait for a disk flush or batch updates to amortise one.

Section II develops the lifecycle and leader-overlap construction. Section III starts with three-voter Crash-Stop-Reincarnation and extends the argument to five voters, stating the history invariants needed for agreement, exclusion of the evicted voter and identity nonreuse. Section VII develops the metadata applications; the accompanying addendum gives the mathematical working and verification evidence. The name “unbounded” follows Turner's treatment of pipelining through reconfiguration: agreement does not depend on a fixed bound on outstanding slots.

## II. Method

### A. Processes, state and identity

The agreement model is asynchronous and non-Byzantine. Messages may be delayed, and an old message retains the identity that issued it. Each incarnation has a logical identity distinct from its physical host. The lifecycle must ensure that an identity never produces new protocol actions after a replacement incarnation takes over.

A clean stop first quiesces protocol processing, then flushes the state required for restart, and finally records a durable stopped marker. On startup, a valid stopped marker permits the process to reload that state and enter *Restarting* with the same identity. Before sending protocol messages it durably records that it is running again. This boot fence prevents a later crash from being mistaken for another clean restart. A restarting process retains its promises and accepted state, so ordinary timeout processing is permitted by the design.

Without a valid clean-stop marker, startup enters *Joining* under a fresh identity. The old identity is crash-stopped. Cached state may reduce transfer work, but it provides no voting authority. The joiner asks the leader to evict the old incarnation and admit the new one; the leader can send missing state while coordinating the change. A joiner does not vote or initiate a view change. Its authority comes from the committed configuration, never from the fact that it shares a host with a former voter.

The implementation design uses replicated superblock markers and majority interpretation of valid marker copies. This paper abstracts that mechanism as correct clean-stop classification and durable identity fencing. The Lean result concerns the resulting lifecycle; it does not model the storage protocol itself.

### B. Quorums across eras

An era determines the quorum families for a suffix of log slots. Write Q^Ie for its phase-one family and Q^IIe for its phase-two family. The history model requires the following within-era and adjacent-era intersections:

q∩ r ≠∅ (q∈Q^IIe, r∈Q^Ie),  (1)

q∩ r ≠∅ (q∈Q^Ie, r∈Q^IIe+1).  (2)

The direction in (2) matters: the earlier phase-one quorum must meet the later decision quorum. These conditions are part of Turner's era-based agreement argument [5].

Our concrete schedule uses the same strict weighted-majority family for both phases. If we(n) is a nonnegative integer weight on a finite support, a quorum q satisfies

2∑n∈ qwe(n)>∑n we(n).  (3)

The weighted proof establishes the requisite intersections when successive configurations differ by at most one unit of total absolute weight. Membership records for zero-weight nodes may change in the same batch without altering (3). A crash itself does not change any weight; only committed configuration commands do.

## III. Three-node Crash-Stop-Reincarnation

Suppose a leader ℓ uses ballot b with an active decision quorum q. To prepare ballot b'>b for the next era, it chooses a prospective phase-one quorum q' with

q∈Q^IIe,q'∈Q^Ie+1,q∩ q'={ℓ}.  (4)

This is Turner's casting-vote condition [5]. The leader first sends prepare requests only to q'∖{ℓ}. Their promises do not disable an acceptor in q. The leader withholds its own promise and may continue proposing at b, provided no other event invalidates the old ballot's guards.

After receiving those promises, the leader chooses the suffix boundary k and makes its own promise locally. Phase one is then complete without another network exchange for the leader's response. Proposals at b' must still obey the normal rule for preserving values reported by phase one. Figure 3 shows the message order.

Decision quorums follow the era of the *slot*, not simply the era associated with a ballot. Consequently, continuing to decide later-era slots at b also requires a quorum valid for those slots. In the concrete pivot below, the active quorum is a majority in both adjoining eras.

The leader stays live for client requests and drives a forced weight sequence from the voting-weights rules [13]: the exiting node's weight goes 1→0 (a Decrement; the Leave follows at weight zero), the new identity joins at weight 0 (a Join), and the new identity goes 0→1. Each step is a unit-weight change, so the strict majorities of consecutive configurations overlap by the unit-change overlap argument and every intermediate era is quorum-safe on its own; a leader dying mid-sequence leaves only safe eras that a new leader can continue. Table 1 instantiates the sequence at unit weight for the three-voter cluster A,B,C with B the leader and C the crashed identity that returns as D: the start configuration is (1,1,1,–); era 1 drives the leader's batch `DECREMENT(C)` + `JOIN(D)`, reaching (1,1,0,0); era 2 drives `INCREMENT(D)` + `LEAVE(C)`, reaching (1,1,–,1). In the table a – is not a weight: it marks an identity that is not a member of the configuration in that era, whereas 0 is the weight of a standby member that the cluster still holds in its order — which the reincarnated node is between the two eras while it is streamed preemptively. The old identity C is evicted by reincarnation, never a recovered amnesiac. If the sequence runs in the doubled scale of a weight-doubling plan, the return halve is integral only when all weights are even, so the corner sequence inserts one extra unit increment on the new identity before the halve. The leader streams the first reconfiguration, then client traffic, and preemptively streams to the reincarnated node as a weight-0 standby; messages to it are fine and messages from it are discarded by the standard membership check. If the leader crashes, the cluster must reach a stable leader before the reincarnated node forces its old identity's eviction.

The addendum published with the code [16] provides a Lean theorem that composes an era-based agreement theorem with replacement weights and an abstract identity lifecycle. The work builds upon Turner's P2–P7 [5]: promises preserve their fences, reports identify prior acceptances, proposals preserve the selected history, and chosen values have quorum evidence. A well-founded total ballot order and the ballot/slot era conditions complete the contract. The addendum states each hypothesis and the exercise that connects operational executions to it.

**Theorem 1 (Three-voter Crash-Stop-Reincarnation).** For a history using Table 1, under the stated order, era and history conditions, any two values chosen for the same slot are equal. The first batch reduces the failed identity to zero voting weight and admits the fresh identity at zero; the second grants the fresh identity unit weight. The retired identity remains without voting authority when subsequent configurations preserve its exclusion. Along the stipulated identity lifecycle, a superseded identity never becomes current again. In a unit-weight three-voter configuration, any leader has a casting vote between two equal one-node minorities; at the promotion boundary, the surviving leader is the singleton overlap shown in Figure 3.

**Proof.** The totals in Table 1 are 3,2,3, so the majority thresholds are 2,2,2. Each boundary changes one weight by one; the integral weighted-overlap argument gives the within-era and adjacent-era intersections. Turner's agreement theorem applies to those intersections and P2–P7. The two commands establish the stated weights, and identity fencing supplies nonreuse. At the promotion boundary, {n1,n2} and {n1,n3} meet only at leader n1; their nonleader members form the two minorities. The leader can collect the replacement's promise while withholding its own. The addendum gives the complete three-node working, including the abstract witness at the first boundary and the state-acquisition obligation.

With all five unit voters responding, a leader has the casting pair {ℓ,a,b} and {ℓ,c,d}. After one voter fails, obtaining the corresponding responding split requires more care. The mechanical transformations in Table 2 organise the weighted replacement while preserving the agreement invariant; the addendum supplies their safety argument and the distinction between abstract witnesses and protocol receipts.

**Theorem 2 (Five-voter Crash-Stop-Reincarnation).** For a history using Table 2, under the same order, era and P2–P7 conditions, any two values chosen for the same slot are equal. After the fourth batch the old identity has zero weight; after the sixth the replacement and all four survivors have unit weight. Preserving exclusion in subsequent configurations and enforcing the identity lifecycle retain the old identity's retirement.

**Proof.** Doubling and exact halving preserve the majority family. Each of the four intervening boundaries changes one unit of voting mass. The same local intersection argument therefore applies six times. Induction over these boundaries establishes the era intersection invariant, and Turner's theorem gives agreement. Evaluation of the commands gives the stated final weights.

## V. Rejoin gossip and witnesses

The replacement schedules of Sections III and IV return a failed member to voting weight through committed batches. They presume the replacement can reach a fence: it must know an era the cluster has committed before its engine sees a message. A node whose durable state is lost returns blank; a node that reincarnates under a fresh identity holds no roster entry at all. Neither can guess an era, and the one-era window drops what it cannot span. The rejoin path closes this with a gossip layer outside the voting protocol; the addendum [16] gives its full construction, its worked scenarios and the kernel-checked safety conditions. Three properties carry the design, none of which touches a quorum rule.

**The join gossip.** A node outside the cluster never assumes the cluster will come to it. It broadcasts a join request to every node it knows, carrying its frontiers, under the deployment's authenticated transport. Any node that hears the request answers with the (era, membership) pair it has committed locally; era advances only when a committed batch is applied, so no honest response names an era the cluster has not committed. The joiner adopts the maximum reported era with that response's membership, flushes the pair to durable storage whenever the adoption changes, and keeps its own retransmission loop: a request lost in flight is re-sent naming the current frontiers, and the resend loop is the gossip's own reliability rather than a separate discovery path. The joiner never assumes it has heard the latest era, so it always sends to all nodes it knows.

**The gossip-witness list.** Every node that hears a join request records the sender on an operator visible witness list; the leader alone acts on the list. It replays the contiguous suffix of its journal from the advertised frontier, in order, each entry through the ordinary accept path — there is no witness-specific accept path — and then streams every subsequent phase-2 and commit to the witness. The witness never votes: quorum membership is decided by the configuration alone. Because the list rides at every node, a successor leader resumes the stream on election without waiting for a re-announcement, so failover does not interrupt keep-up. When a committed batch makes the witness a member, every node removes the entry; delivery as both voter and witness in the window between the commit and the removal is covered by deduplication and merely wastes IO. A node registered as a witness in the startup configuration is never purged: it is an append-only audit tape of everything the cluster committed, and a disaster-recovery sink in another region costs exactly the stream that reincarnation already needs, under every leader in turn.

**Why the stream is safe.** The stream telescopes a promise: per Lamport, a promise at ballot b covers every lower ballot, so a phase-2 accepted at view v raises the witness's promise floor to v exactly as if a prepare at v had arrived first. The witness forfeits nothing by raising the floor — its promises are counted in no quorum — and the leader's right to send at v was earned from members by the ordinary phase one. The addendum states this as five construction rules, H1–H5, and proves the consequence in Lean: the adopted era never exceeds the committed era; once the leader answers, it equals it; a journal extended by the in-order contiguous suffix reconstructs the leader's prefix; and the promoted witness's committed prefix is indistinguishable from a member that caught up by ordinary state transfer. A node that rejoins through this path arrives at its first fence at the cluster's current era, having seen a commit, which makes the one-era window gate unreachable by construction rather than widened.

## VII. Discussion

### A. Applications

An application of strong consistency is the replication of small state by consensus: the configuration authority Lamport, Malkhi and Zhou describe [14], and Vertical Paxos's auxiliary configuration master [15], are the precedents. This design embeds such an authority in the application — for small metadata, with ordinary replication performed in memory.

**Short-lived advisory locks and leader leases.** A replicated state machine can order acquisition, renewal and expiry decisions over a small record containing an owner, generation and expiry policy. An expiry command for an old generation must not revoke a newer grant. If a client uses ownership to control an external resource, that resource may also need to reject stale generations. A real-time leader lease additionally needs explicit clock and timing assumptions.

**A membership authority for another algorithm.** An application whose bulk algorithm uses gossip or another weaker consistency model may still need one agreed version of cluster membership, ownership assignments or configuration changes. An embedded replicated state machine can supply that authority, while the bulk algorithm disseminates or consumes its decisions.

**The configuration as replicated state.** In the Paxos family the configuration itself is replicated consensus state: Lamport, Malkhi and Zhou define a reconfigurable state machine whose configuration is a state component changed only by an executed reconfiguration command [14], and Vertical Paxos instead commits each new configuration to an auxiliary configuration master, which determines the set of acceptors of every configuration [15]. This design keeps that commitment inside the application: reconfiguration batches commit in the same slot stream as client values.

**The CORFU precedent.** CORFU provides a concrete precedent for compact configuration metadata alongside a larger storage system [7]. Its reconfiguration is patterned after Vertical Paxos; Section 2.5 describes storage units acting as acceptors in a Paxos-like protocol. Section 2.6 estimates that adding 0.1% writes keeps projection metadata below 25 MB for a 1 TB cluster under an adversarial workload. The described implementation uses a shared network drive for agreed projections.

**External coordination services.** ZooKeeper and etcd are established ways to supply coordination separately from an application. ZooKeeper offers linearizable state changes and per-client ordering, while local reads have weaker semantics [8]. etcd documents strict serializability for its key-value operations and distinguishes default linearizable reads from optional serializable reads [9]. An application must choose the operation semantics it actually needs.

A separately operated service also brings deployment and resource costs. For scale, etcd's version 3.6 hardware guide gives a small-cluster example with dedicated two-vCPU, 8 GB AWS machines [10]. etcd can itself be embedded in a Go application [11]. The proposed combination here is small application-owned state, volatile normal-path replication, and replacement through fresh identities and overlapping quorums.

### B. Scope

**Implementation correspondence.** The theorems assume protocol-history invariants. Their composition with the era-changing operational model is left as an exercise to the reader. The addendum [16] makes that exercise verifiable by stating its mapping, base case and preservation obligations, including clean-stop classification, identity fencing and state transfer before voting.

**Scope and availability.** Tables 1 and 2 replace one voter among three and five respectively. The addendum constructs paths between arbitrary weighted-majority endpoints with available majorities. Agreement follows from the history contract; progress additionally uses available recipients and a stable leader. Fresh identities permit repeated replacement while each committed boundary preserves the invariant.

**Meaning of strong consistency.** The mechanised result is per-slot agreement. A complete service-level linearizability argument also needs ordered execution, appropriate read semantics and a mapping between client operations and log entries. Lease guarantees require the additional conditions stated above.

## VIII. The demonstration: lunet-locks

The demonstration is `lunet-locks`, a runnable, fixed-membership advisory-lock service. It orders lock requests with `vrr-core`, the sans-IO Viewstamped Replication Revisited core that accompanies this formalisation: one total function maps a tick, an input message, and a state to a new state and the messages to send, and the host owns time, transport, storage, packetisation, threading, and naming. Clients speak newline-delimited JSON over TCP; replicas exchange the core's binary datagrams over raw UDP; the service process is LuaJIT and libuv (the Lunet runtime, version-pinned and hash-verified), and a small Rust adapter drives the core and owns the lock state machine.

### A. Components

The adapter instantiates the concrete core replica over a segmented log with weighted-majority quorums, running volatile stability: protocol state lives in quorum memory, an empty journal is provisioned at boot, and the only bytes the service ever fsyncs locally are the recovery nonce file. The adapter retains the tick clock (a monotonic value clamped against wall-clock regression), exactly-once reply correlation keyed by the client's message identifier, and guard checks that re-validate every replicated operation entry — in Prepare, DoViewChange, StartView, RecoveryResponse, and NewState messages — against the lock service before the entry reaches the core. The Teal/Lunet wrapper owns sockets, TCP framing, peer source validation, and forwarding; a UDP peer envelope carrying a membership fingerprint guards the fixed membership at the transport boundary, so differently configured clusters cannot merge. An append-only lock-event journal, a REST/WebSocket feed, and a console are observability only: the journal never participates in replication or recovery, and a journal error disables journaling without affecting the service path.

### B. The rejoin path implemented

Every boot enters fenced recovery stamped with a fresh durable nonce. The nonce file is updated atomically — write, fsync, rename, parent-directory sync — and the new value is used as the recovery input's tick, so per-node monotonicity holds across the two clock sources. Recovery needs a live quorum: a restarted node rejoins from quorum memory, installing the latest fenced view's history and re-applying its committed suffix. A rolling single-node restart therefore recovers from the surviving members, while a simultaneous full-cluster loss forfeits whatever the quorum held, and an operator re-bootstrapping a lost cluster must do so only after all outstanding leases can no longer be valid.

A node outside the cluster — a lost durable state or a fresh identity — rejoins through the gossip path of Section V: the join gossip to every known node, the witness list acted on by the leader, and the streamed keep-up to the first fence. The wire carries the gossip as its own message type, and the explicit-message tests pin the whole exchange; the section's safety argument is the contract those tests hold the implementation to.

Membership is fixed for one process lifetime, so the reincarnation eviction of Section II — the forced weight sequence that replaces the crashed identity with a new one — is not exercised by this service. What the demonstration implements is the classic recovery boundary whose amnesia exposure motivates reincarnation, hardened with durable-nonce fencing: the fresh nonce is exactly the freshness ingress that the reincarnation design's stale-episode rule realises, and a restarting node can never complete recovery with a stale identity stamp. Converting the eviction sequence into this service is the next integration step.

### C. The lock and lease API

The client protocol has three operations, one JSON object per line over TCP. Every request carries a message identifier, a stable client identifier, an increasing request number, and a lock identifier; retries reuse the exact envelope, and replication deduplicates by (client, request number), replaying the prior reply. `get` returns the live lease on a lock, or null. `set` grants or renews a lease — a lease identifier, a holder identifier, and an expiry — and a lease is live only when its expiry is later than the execution time; an expired candidate is rejected, an absent or expired incumbent is free, and a live incumbent may be renewed or replaced only by the same holder. `release` removes a live lease only when both holder and lease identifier match, and is idempotent for a missing or expired lease. Lease expiry is what makes the diskless design safe to operate: a crashed holder's locks free themselves when the lease lapses, without any recovery path trusting the holder's lost state.

### D. Test and hardening status

The service is exercised from several directions: a pure-Teal test suite and an FFI-boundary test suite for the lock service and cluster configuration; a Cerulean format gate and Cyan type checks; the Rust adapter's checks, tests, and release build, which depend on the exact pinned `vrr-core` git revision fetched over HTTPS; a three-process smoke test covering acquire, read, contention, release, reacquisition, expiry takeover, and a one-replica restart with a live quorum; and a thirty-second, three-datacentre lease-failover simulation that kills the observed lease holder every three seconds and verifies takeover within five seconds, exiting nonzero on a conflicting holder. The same simulation runs against a stable Docker cluster. CI runs on Linux for regular changes and a full platform matrix with packaged releases for tagged versions.

It is a working implementation under active hardening and testing, not a finished demonstration of the reincarnation protocol.

### B. E2: disk modes at the reincarnation boundary

What does the diskless default save, in latency, against paying a forced flush at the reincarnation boundary? The design builds custom `lunet-locks` binaries, identical in every respect other than the durability behaviour at the reincarnation boundary — the point at which a reincarnating node durably records state before rejoining serving:

- *Diskless (the default).* No durable writes at the reincarnation boundary; quorum memory is the only protocol state, and the recovery nonce file remains as shipped.
- *Naive single write (the latency baseline).* One 4 KiB block write followed by `fsync` at the reincarnation boundary. Per the FAST'18 finding [4], stock consensus systems lose committed data under a single storage fault despite `fsync`; the variant is the latency baseline only, never a safety claim.
- *Double-ring write.* A TigerBeetle-style double write at the reincarnation boundary: one 4 KiB block of data plus a checksum header to the first WAL ring, then the header and checksum with no payload to the second WAL ring, the two rings spaced apart in separate 4 KiB-sector-aligned zones so the two checksum copies sit in different erasure blocks. Both flush variants carry fake data only, with no read-back: they measure write-and-flush latency and never reconstruct state from what was written.

### C. E3: Maelstrom brute force at three and five nodes

### E. E5: the failover budget — N round trips, no disk flush

The design claim is that view failover costs N network round trips and *no* forced disk flush, where the classic diskful alternatives pay at least one flush on the fence path. The budget is measured, not assumed: the view change is decomposed into its wire segments and each segment timed. For the three-node cluster the segments are: suspicion (the phi-accrued suspect interval, floored by the host's election knob), StartViewChange dissemination (1 RTT), DoViewChange collection at the next view's primary (pipelined with dissemination: 0 extra RTT when the messages cross), and StartView installation (1 RTT): the designed expectation is failover = suspicion floor + 2 RTT + 0 flushes. The counterfactual arm pays the same suspicion floor and round trips *plus* a forced flush before fencing (the VSR-1988-class stable-storage barrier) and a further flush before the new primary may serve installed state: suspicion floor + flush + 2 RTT + flush. Both arms are measured on the same hardware, the same run. The counterfactual is built by the forced-write rig of the campaign plan: a custom test build of the demonstration performs one 4 KiB write and `fsync` *outside* the algorithm — before the fenced node emits its view-change traffic and again before the new primary answers StartView — so the rig prices the diskful barrier without any uVRR code being made unsafe or rewritten to resemble the compared systems. The reported numbers are the per-segment medians and the totals: suspicion floor X.XX ms; per-RTT X.XX ms; total failover, ours, X.XX ms; total failover, forced-flush arm, XX.X ms; flush cost per boundary X.XX ms, expressed also in units of the same-run RTT, X.X RTTs. The last ratio is the hardware-agnostic headline: it should survive moving between loopback, local virtual machines, and cloud machines, where the absolute latencies will not. ## X. Related Work

Viewstamped Replication introduced the organisation of replication around views and a primary [1]; VRR revisits that organisation and separates normal operation, view change, recovery and reconfiguration [2]. Michael *et al.* identify the failure of diskless recovery to preserve view-change commitments and construct virtual stable storage [3]. uVRR takes a different route: it makes a crash final for the old identity, then brings the process back as a new member through reconfiguration. The replacement schedule connects that lifecycle to agreement across configurations.

Turner's *Unbounded Pipelining in Dynamically Reconfigurable Paxos Clusters* supplies the central quorum geometry, era-based agreement argument and casting-vote construction [5]. Withholding the leader's own promise is Turner's mechanism. The present work applies it to reincarnation, proves weighted configuration reachability and an abstract casting-vote completion, and proves agreement for a concrete replacement instantiation from the protocol's history invariants. The application discussion places this design alongside coordination services and CORFU, using their stated semantics and resource context.
